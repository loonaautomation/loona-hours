How It Works — The Rule Behind This Calculator

A shift differential is extra pay per hour for nights, evenings or weekends. The rule most workers (and some payroll setups) miss: under the FLSA, overtime must be paid at 1.5× the regular rate, and the regular rate includes differentials. The legal method is:

  • Straight-time earnings = (all hours × base rate) + (differential hours × premium).
  • Regular (blended) rate = straight-time earnings ÷ total hours.
  • Overtime premium = overtime hours × 0.5 × blended rate, added on top (the straight-time part of those hours is already inside straight-time earnings).

Worked example: 40 hours at $20 with 16 night hours at +$2, plus 5 overtime day hours. Straight-time = 45×$20 + 16×$2 = $932; blended rate = $932 ÷ 45 = $20.71; overtime premium = 5 × $10.36 = $51.78; gross = $983.78. Paying overtime on the bare $20 base instead would short the week by about $1.78 — small per week, real money per year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a shift differential?

Extra pay per hour for working less desirable shifts — nights, evenings, weekends or holidays. It is usually a flat amount (for example +$2/hour) or a percentage of the base rate. This calculator uses the flat-amount form; for a percentage, convert it to dollars first (10% of $20 = $2).

Must overtime be paid on the differential too?

Yes. Federal law defines the 'regular rate' to include shift differentials and most other premiums, so overtime is 1.5× the blended rate, not 1.5× the base rate alone.

My stub shows overtime at 1.5× my base rate only. Is that wrong?

If you also earned a differential in that week, very likely yes — the overtime premium should be based on the blended rate. Raise it with payroll with your week's hours and differential hours listed; this page's breakdown is a good printout to bring.

Do differentials count in weeks with no overtime?

They still add their per-hour premium to straight-time earnings (that part is in every week). They only change the overtime maths in weeks past 40 hours.

How it works — the formula

When part of a week is paid at a higher rate, overtime is not priced off the base rate alone. This calculator adds up the week’s straight-time earnings — every hour at the base rate, plus the premium on the hours you mark as differential hours — and divides by the total hours to get the blended regular rate. Overtime then adds the missing half (0.5×) of that blended rate for each hour over 40.

The formula:
Straight-time earnings = (total hours × base rate) + (differential hours × premium per hour)
Blended regular rate = straight-time earnings ÷ total hours
Overtime premium = hours over 40 × blended rate × 0.5
Gross pay = straight-time earnings + overtime premium

Worked example

Your base rate is $22/hour with a $3/hour night premium. Monday to Friday you work 8 hours a day, 6 of them at the premium, and you add a 4-hour day shift on Saturday. The week totals 44 hours, 30 of them differential hours. Straight-time earnings: 44 × $22 + 30 × $3 = $968 + $90 = $1,058.00. Blended regular rate: $1,058 ÷ 44 = $24.05. Overtime premium: 4 hours × $24.05 × 0.5 = $48.09. Gross pay: $1,106.09 — exactly what the calculator shows for these inputs.

Frequently asked questions

Why isn’t my overtime simply 1.5 × ($22 + $3) = $37.50 an hour?

Because the premium attaches to only some of the week’s hours. The regular rate used for overtime is the week’s weighted average — total straight-time earnings divided by total hours — which in the example is $24.05, not $25.00. Applying $37.50 to every overtime hour would pay the night premium on hours that never earned it; applying 1.5× to the $22 base alone would underpay it.

My premium is a percentage of my base rate, not a flat amount. How do I enter it?

Convert it to dollars per hour first. A 10% premium on a $22 base rate is $2.20 per differential hour — enter 2.20 in the premium box, and the calculator treats it exactly like any other flat premium.

I work two different base rates in the same week. Can this page handle that?

This calculator accepts one base rate plus one premium. The blended-rate idea is the same with two base rates — total straight-time earnings divided by total hours — but you would have to total the earnings from both rates yourself before the overtime half can be priced. As a rough check, a single blended figure entered here gets you close.

Does the order of my shifts matter — nights early in the week, days later?

No. The blend is built from the week’s totals: total hours, total differential hours, total straight-time earnings. Which specific hours crossed the 40-hour line does not change the blended rate or the overtime premium in this calculation.

Is the differential premium taxed differently from my regular pay?

No — the differential and the overtime premium are ordinary wages, and the figure this page shows is gross pay before any taxes or withholdings. What is actually withheld is a payroll matter governed by tax law and your W-4, not by this calculator.
