Minutes to Decimal Hours — The Complete Guide With the Full Chart

Why payroll thinks in hundredths, the one-line formula, the full 1–60 chart, and the rounding rules that decide whether the chart helps or hurts you.

In short: Decimal hours = minutes ÷ 60. 15 minutes is 0.25, 30 is 0.50, 45 is 0.75 — and every pay calculation in the country is done in these decimals, not in clock time.

Why payroll thinks in decimals

Clocks speak in sixtieths; payroll speaks in hundredths. A shift of 7 hours 45 minutes is natural to say as “7:45,” but you cannot multiply 7:45 by an hourly rate until it becomes 7.75 hours. That conversion — minutes divided by 60 — is all that “decimal hours” means, and it sits underneath every digital timesheet, time-clock export and pay stub in the country.

The stakes are small per shift and large per year. Seven minutes is 0.12 hours; priced at $20, that is $2.40. Misround in the same direction on 250 shifts and you have moved $600 — which is why labour law cares about rounding conventions and why workers should know the chart.

The formula and the anchors

Decimal hours = hours + (minutes ÷ 60). Going back: decimal part × 60 = minutes. Four anchors cover most mental math:

  • 15 minutes = 0.25 — a quarter hour.
  • 30 minutes = 0.50 — a half hour.
  • 45 minutes = 0.75 — three quarters.
  • Every 6 minutes = 0.10 — a tenth of an hour, the unit many time clocks report in.

Worked example: a shift from 9:07 AM to 5:52 PM is 8 hours 45 minutes = 8 + 45/60 = 8.75 hours. At $18/hour that shift pays 8.75 × $18 = $157.50 before any overtime split. Reverse example: 6.35 hours is 6 hours + 0.35 × 60 = 6 hours 21 minutes.

Rounding rules in the real world

Employers commonly round punches before converting: to the nearest quarter hour (15 minutes), or under the 7-minute rule, where 1–7 minutes past a quarter rounds down and 8–14 rounds up. Federal law permits rounding only when it works out neutrally over time — it may not systematically shave minutes in the employer's favour. Some systems instead convert exact minutes and round only the final pay amount to the cent, which is the most accurate method of all.

If you keep your own log (you should), record exact clock times. You can always apply the chart or a rounding rule later; you can never recover exactness from a log that was rounded as it was written.

Six-minute increments: the tenth-of-an-hour system

Some industries — law firms, consultancies, many healthcare and IT contractors — bill and pay in tenths of an hour: 0.1 = 6 minutes. The tenth chart is short enough to memorise: 6 min = 0.1, 12 = 0.2, 18 = 0.3, 24 = 0.4, 30 = 0.5, 36 = 0.6, 42 = 0.7, 48 = 0.8, 54 = 0.9.

Systems working in tenths usually round each entry to the nearest tenth, so a 4-minute task records as 0.1 (never 0.0 if any time was worked and the system rounds half up) and a 9-minute call as 0.2. The same federal neutrality principle applies as with quarter-hour rounding: rounding must not systematically favour one side. Across a month of small entries, one-directional tenth-rounding moves real money — 3 minutes shaved from 20 entries a week is an hour a month.

If your employer reports in tenths and you log in h:mm, convert with the 6-minute anchors above and expect small, explainable differences of ±0.05 hours per entry. Differences bigger than that, or all leaning one way, are worth a question.

Decimal hours in overtime math

Overtime splits are computed on decimal totals: a week of 42.50 decimal hours at $20 is 40 × $20 + 2.50 × $30 = $875. Breaks convert the same way — an unpaid 30-minute break is 0.50 hours off the day. When you verify a stub, convert each day first, then add the week: stub totals are decimal, and comparing your h:mm notes directly against them is how phantom discrepancies appear.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert minutes to decimal hours?

Divide the minutes by 60 and add any whole hours. 45 minutes = 0.75 hours; 2 hours 20 minutes = 2.33 hours. The chart on this page covers every minute from 1 to 60.

Why is 7 minutes 0.12 and not 0.11 or 0.07?

Because decimals are fractions of an hour, not minutes with a dot: 7 ÷ 60 = 0.1166…, which rounds to 0.12. Writing “7:07” as 7.07 hours is the classic beginner error — 7.07 hours is actually 7 hours and about 4 minutes.

What is 0.35 hours in minutes?

Multiply the decimal part by 60: 0.35 × 60 = 21 minutes, so 0.35 hours is 21 minutes and 6.35 hours is 6 hours 21 minutes.

Does rounding minutes cost me money?

Fair rounding is neutral over time — it must average out under federal rules. One-directional rounding (always down on clock-in, always down on clock-out length) is not fair rounding and is not allowed.

Should my timesheet use h:mm or decimal?

Record in h:mm (exact clock times) and let payroll convert. If you submit decimals directly, use the standard two-decimal chart so your numbers match the payroll system's.
