How to Fill Out a Timesheet Correctly — And Check It Against Your Pay Stub

The five entries that matter, the rounding rule nobody explains, and the three-number weekly check that catches payroll errors.

What a timesheet is really for

A timesheet looks like paperwork, but it is better understood as the evidence your pay is calculated from. Federal law requires employers to keep records of hours worked and wages paid for at least two years (three for payroll records) — and when a dispute arises, the side with the contemporaneous record usually wins. A timesheet you fill in honestly, every day, is the cheapest insurance an hourly worker can hold.

The good news: a correct timesheet needs only five things per shift — the date, when you started, when you stopped, how long your unpaid break was, and anything unusual that happened. Everything else (totals, overtime splits, gross pay) is arithmetic a calculator can do better than a tired human on a Friday.

The five entries, done right

  • 1. Record actual times, not scheduled times. If the schedule says 9–5 but you worked 8:50–5:20, the timesheet says 8:50–5:20. “Schedule equals timesheet” is how overtime quietly evaporates.
  • 2. Use one clock convention and stick to it. Write AM/PM explicitly or use 24-hour time. Most timesheet errors are not math errors — they are 7:00-something errors, especially on overnight shifts.
  • 3. Log breaks as they happened. Note the meal break's length; if you worked through lunch, write “no break — worked” rather than leaving the standard 30 minutes to be auto-deducted.
  • 4. Mark overnight shifts clearly. A shift from 10 PM to 6 AM belongs to the day it started in most payroll systems. Consistency matters more than the convention; surprises come from mixing them.
  • 5. Initial changes. If you correct an entry, correct it openly (single line through, initial, rewrite) rather than erasing. An altered-looking timesheet loses arguments a plain one wins.

Rounding: the part nobody explains

Employers may round punches to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour, or quarter hour, provided rounding averages out and does not systematically favour the employer. The classic 7-minute rule rounds within a quarter hour: 1–7 minutes past the quarter rounds down, 8–14 rounds up. Rounding is legal; shaving — always rounding in the employer's favour — is not.

What this means practically: record your exact times anyway. If payroll rounds, the rounded figures should cluster around your exact ones over the month. If every single rounding goes one way, that pattern is itself evidence. Our time rounding calculator shows both standard methods side by side.

Totals and the weekly check

Once the entries exist, the weekly routine is mechanical: convert each shift to paid minutes (out minus in minus unpaid break), add the week, and split at 40 hours for overtime in most states — or at your state's daily thresholds if you work in California, Alaska, Colorado, Nevada or Oregon. That is exactly what the time card calculator automates, including print and CSV export, and the per-state time cards on this site apply each state's own rule.

  • Compare three numbers with every pay stub: total paid hours, overtime hours, and the overtime rate. Totals can look right while the split is wrong — two errors cancel.
  • Check that the overtime rate is 1.5× your regular rate including differentials and non-discretionary bonuses, not just the base wage.
  • Confirm your week boundary. If your workweek starts Sunday and you count Monday–Sunday, your split will differ from payroll's without anyone being dishonest — ask which day the workweek starts.

Timesheet apps and digital systems: what changes, what does not

Most timesheets are now apps — geofenced clocks, badge swipes, scheduling platforms that double as time clocks. The technology changes the failure modes, not the principles. Digital systems rarely lose a punch, but they happily encode wrong rules: an auto-lunch you never took, a rounding setting tilted one way, a workweek boundary you never chose.

Three digital-era habits protect you. First, screenshot your week before submitting it — after approval, many systems show you only the approved version. Second, learn where the app keeps the unrounded punches; most systems store exact times underneath rounded displays, and those exact times are what a wage claim is built from. Third, treat schedule-app messages (“can you stay till 7?”) as part of your record: they corroborate the extra hours your log claims.

And the oldest rule survives every platform: if the system is down, write the times in a notebook or a phone note and enter them when it returns, noting that you did. A reconstruction made the same day is a record; one made at month's end is a guess.

Paper, app or spreadsheet — what actually matters

The medium matters less than three habits: fill it in the same day (memory of Tuesday's times is fiction by Friday), keep your own copy (a photo of the paper sheet, the app's export, or our Save-My-Week storage on your own phone), and keep it for years, not weeks — wage claims can reach back two to three years, and your copy is what makes an old claim provable.

If your employer provides no timesheet at all — common in small businesses and domestic work — start your own today. A notebook, a notes app or a weekly screenshot of a filled calculator page all work. Courts and labor departments accept a worker's own reconstructed records when the employer kept none; the legal burden then shifts to the employer to disprove them.

When the timesheet and the stub disagree

Start specific and in writing: “Week of October 12: I recorded 46.5 hours including 6.5 overtime; the stub pays 42 and 2. Here is my log.” Most payroll errors are genuine errors and get corrected at this step. If they are not, your state labor department's wage-claim process is free, and the federal Wage and Hour Division takes complaints at no cost. Retaliation for raising a wage question is illegal under the FLSA — one more reason to keep the exchange written and factual.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I keep my timesheets?

Keep your own copies for at least three years. Federal wage claims generally reach back two years (three for willful violations), and state windows can be longer — your records are what makes an older claim provable.

Can my employer change my timesheet?

Employers may correct genuine errors, but they cannot delete hours you actually worked. If entries change, your own copy is the comparison that matters — which is why you should keep one, with photos of paper sheets.

Should I record breaks even if they are auto-deducted?

Yes — especially when you worked through lunch. Auto-deduction assumes the break happened; your note “no break — worked” is the evidence that overrides the assumption.

What if I forgot to clock in?

Write the actual start time as soon as you notice and tell your supervisor the same day. An honest same-day correction is a non-event; discovering it at payroll time turns it into a dispute about memory.

Is a photo of my schedule enough?

A schedule shows what was planned, not what was worked. It helps as supporting evidence, but a daily log of actual in/out times — even in a phone note — is far stronger.
