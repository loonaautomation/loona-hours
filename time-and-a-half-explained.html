Time and a Half, Explained — Meaning, Formula & Rate Table

What the phrase means in law and on your stub, how to compute it for any wage, and when employers actually owe it.

In short: Time and a half = 1.5 × your regular rate — your normal pay plus a 50% premium. Federal law owes it on every hour past 40 in a workweek, calculated on your full regular rate (differentials and bonuses included).

What “time and a half” means

“Time and a half” is plain language for a pay rate of 1.5 times your regular hourly rate: your normal rate, plus a half-rate premium on top. If you earn $20 an hour, time and a half is $30 an hour — $20 of ordinary pay and $10 of premium. It is the standard overtime rate in the United States: federal law requires it for every hour a non-exempt employee works beyond 40 in a workweek.

The phrase is much older than the law — it comes from early union contracts — but since the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 it has had a precise legal meaning, calculated on the regular rate: all of your remuneration for the week divided by the hours worked, including shift differentials and non-discretionary bonuses. Time and a half of your base wage, when you also earn a differential, is actually less than the law requires.

The formula and three quick examples

Time-and-a-half rate = regular rate × 1.5. Overtime pay = overtime hours × that rate. Weekly gross for a week with overtime = (40 × regular rate) + (overtime hours × regular rate × 1.5).

  • $15/hour: time and a half is $22.50. A 45-hour week pays 40 × $15 + 5 × $22.50 = $712.50.
  • $20/hour: time and a half is $30.00. A 50-hour week pays $800 + 10 × $30 = $1,100.
  • $24.60/hour: time and a half is $36.90. Six overtime hours pay 6 × $36.90 = $221.40 on top of regular pay.

Two pay rates in one week: the blended regular rate

Many workers hold two rates in the same week — host on weekdays at $16, bartender on weekends at $18. Overtime law handles this with the weighted-average regular rate: add all straight-time earnings for the week, divide by all hours worked, and that average becomes the rate the 1.5× is built on.

Worked example: 30 hours at $16 ($480) plus 15 hours at $18 ($270) is $750 over 45 hours — a regular rate of $16.67. The 5 overtime hours have already been paid at straight time inside the $750, so the law adds the premium half: 5 × $8.33 = $41.67, for a legal gross of $791.67. (If the employer instead pays 1.5× the rate of the job being worked during the overtime hours, that is allowed only under a written agreement made before the work is performed.)

This is also why “time and a half of what?” is the right first question about any overtime line: the answer is a blend of everything you earned that week, not necessarily the rate printed next to the overtime row.

When time and a half applies — and when it doesn't

  • Applies: hours over 40 in a workweek for non-exempt employees (the default for hourly workers); daily overtime hours in CA, AK, CO, NV and OR under their state rules.
  • Does not automatically apply: weekends, nights and holidays as such — federal law prices the week's total, not the calendar. Premiums for those are employer policy unless a state law or contract says otherwise.
  • Not owed to exempt employees — bona fide executive, administrative and professional staff who meet the duties and salary tests, plus specific categories like teachers and outside sales.
  • Public sector difference: government employers may offer compensating time off at 1.5 hours per overtime hour instead of cash; private employers generally may not.

Time and a half vs double time

Double time (2×) is time and a half's bigger sibling. Federally it is never required — it appears in California's daily rules (after 12 hours in a day, and after 8 on a seventh consecutive day), in some union contracts, and in many employers' holiday policies. When people say “I get double time on Sundays,” that is almost always a company policy or contract term, not a statute — which also means it can be renegotiated in a way statutory overtime cannot.

One more distinction worth knowing: some employers pay the premium half only on top of a salary that already covers straight time for all hours (the “fluctuating workweek” and fixed-salary arrangements). The visible rate then looks like 0.5× for overtime hours — legal only under specific conditions, and a frequent source of disputes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate time and a half?

Multiply your regular hourly rate by 1.5. At $18/hour, time and a half is $27/hour. Then multiply by the number of overtime hours to get the overtime pay for those hours.

Is time and a half taxed differently?

No. Overtime earnings are ordinary income taxed at the same rates. A large overtime week may be withheld more heavily in that paycheck, but your annual tax calculation evens it out.

Do I get time and a half for working weekends?

Not under federal law by itself. Weekend hours pay time and a half only when they push the workweek past 40 hours — or when your employer's policy, a contract, or a state daily-overtime rule grants it.

What is time and a half on $20 an hour?

$30.00 an hour. A 45-hour week at $20 therefore pays $800 regular + $150 overtime = $950 gross.

Can my employer pay time and a half as time off?

Private employers generally cannot substitute time off for overtime pay. Government employers may offer comp time at 1.5 hours per overtime hour under the FLSA's public-sector rules.
